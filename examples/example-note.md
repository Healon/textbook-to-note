> [!summary] Key points
> - The shoulder (glenohumeral) joint trades bony stability for range of
>   motion — the glenoid covers only about a third of the humeral head
>   surface (`OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology Ch.9`)
> - Static stability comes from the labrum and capsuloligamentous complex;
>   dynamic stability comes from the rotator cuff and scapular stabilizers
>   (`OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology Ch.9`)
> - It is the most mobile — and most frequently dislocated — major joint in
>   the body (`OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology Ch.9`)

# Background

## Definition
- A ball-and-socket synovial joint between the head of the humerus and the
  glenoid fossa of the scapula (`OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology Ch.9`)

## Structure
- Glenoid fossa is shallow and only articulates with roughly a third of the
  humeral head's surface area — the fibrocartilaginous **glenoid labrum**
  deepens the socket and increases contact area (`OpenStax Anatomy &
  Physiology Ch.9`)
	- *Anterior view of the right shoulder showing the glenoid fossa, labrum,
	  and humeral head — `OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology Ch.9 Fig.9.13`*
	- ![[Shoulder_Fig9-13_OpenStax_glenohumeral-joint.png|450]]
- The joint capsule is reinforced by the glenohumeral ligaments (superior,
  middle, inferior) and the coracohumeral ligament (`OpenStax Anatomy &
  Physiology Ch.9`)

## Mechanism (stability trade-off)
- ==Bony stability is deliberately sacrificed for mobility==: the shallow
  socket permits flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation, and
  circumduction — the widest range of motion of any joint (`OpenStax
  Anatomy & Physiology Ch.9`)
- Stability is instead provided by two systems:
	- **Static stabilizers** — labrum, joint capsule, glenohumeral ligaments
	- **Dynamic stabilizers** — rotator cuff muscles (supraspinatus,
	  infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) compress the humeral head
	  into the glenoid throughout motion (`OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology
	  Ch.9`)

# Evaluation

## Assessment methods

### History
- Mechanism of injury and direction of instability (anterior vs posterior
  vs multidirectional) guide the differential (`OpenStax Anatomy &
  Physiology Ch.9`)

### Physical exam
- Apprehension and relocation testing assess anterior instability;
  jerk test assesses posterior instability (`OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology
  Ch.9`)

## Classification
- Instability is commonly classified by direction (anterior / posterior /
  multidirectional) and by cause (traumatic vs atraumatic) — see the
  comparison table below

| Category | Typical mechanism | Key exam finding |
|---|---|---|
| Traumatic anterior | Forced abduction + external rotation | Positive apprehension test |
| Atraumatic multidirectional | Generalized ligamentous laxity | Positive sulcus sign |
| Posterior | Posteriorly directed force on flexed, adducted arm | Positive jerk test |

# Application

## ⭐ Assessment-to-management overview
> [!note] Overview
> - Direction of instability (from history + exam above) determines which
>   provocative tests and which conservative-vs-surgical pathway apply —
>   see `[[#Classification]]` for the driving categories

## Conservative approach
### Rotator cuff strengthening
- Dynamic stabilizer training is the first-line approach for atraumatic and
  multidirectional instability, given the joint's reliance on muscular
  rather than bony stability (`OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology Ch.9`)
